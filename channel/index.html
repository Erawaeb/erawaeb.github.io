Scam Shield.

The complete scam-defense playbook. Spot it, verify it, survive it.

Scams aren't about stupidity — they're about pressure. Every scam, from the sketchy text to the polished "investment advisor," runs the same playbook: rush you, scare you, and keep you from thinking clearly. This guide gives you the tells, the checklist, and the recovery plan.

PART 1The five tells

If a message hits two or more of these, treat it as hostile until proven otherwise:

  1. Urgency. "Act now," "account suspended," "offer expires today." Urgency is the scammer's favorite weapon because it shuts down your thinking.
  2. Authority. It claims to be your bank, the IRS, the police, a delivery company. Authority makes you obey first and question later.
  3. Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone," "keep this between us." Legitimate organizations never ask you to hide the conversation.
  4. Weird payment. Gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, payment apps to strangers. Real businesses don't take iTunes cards.
  5. Contact out of nowhere. You didn't apply for the job, enter the contest, or miss the delivery — but somehow they found you.

PART 2The verify-before-you-trust checklist

Run this before you click, reply, or pay — it takes ninety seconds:

  • Stop and breathe. Urgency is manufactured. A real problem will still be there in ten minutes.
  • Check the sender. Look at the actual email address or number, not the display name. One letter off is all it takes.
  • Go direct. Don't use their link or number. Open the company's app or site yourself, or call the number on your card.
  • Search the script. Paste the message's exact wording into a search engine — scams are mass-produced, and someone has flagged it.
  • Ask a human. Forward it to a friend or family member. Scams survive in isolation and die in daylight.

PART 3If you already clicked

It happens. What matters is speed now — every minute counts:

  1. Disconnect. If you downloaded something, disconnect from Wi-Fi. If you entered a password, change it immediately — from a different device if you can.
  2. Call your bank. If any card or account details were involved, call the number on the back of your card and tell them what happened. They deal with this daily.
  3. Lock it down. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere important, and freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — it's free and takes ten minutes.
  4. Report it. File a report at reportfraud.ftc.gov (US). Reporting helps investigators connect cases — and it's how patterns get caught.

PART 4If you already sent money

Be honest with yourself about recovery odds — they depend on how you paid:

  • Credit card: best odds. Call your issuer and dispute the charge immediately.
  • Debit card / bank transfer: call your bank right away. Faster is better; wires can sometimes be recalled within hours.
  • Payment apps (Zelle, Cash App, Venmo): harder. Report it in the app and to your bank, but treat sent money as likely gone.
  • Gift cards / crypto: effectively unrecoverable. Report it anyway — and treat anyone who contacts you offering to "recover" it for a fee as a second scam. Recovery scams are real.

The one rule: slow is smooth, smooth is safe. Every scam needs your speed. Take it away, and the whole thing falls apart.
