THE BE AWARE DIGEST DAILY RED FLAG #002 — SEP 15, 2026

THE SCAM

The near miss that keeps you spinning.

Two reels line up matching symbols. The third stops just one click short of the jackpot. Your heart jumps — you were so close. It feels like the machine is warming up, like the big one is next.

It isn't. The outcome was locked in before the reels ever moved: a random number generator picked it, and the spin is just a show. Many machines are built to land 'near misses' far more often than real randomness would — weighted reels that make 'almost' feel constant. As the psychologist B.F. Skinner wrote back in 1953, almost hitting the jackpot makes people keep playing, 'although this reinforcement costs the owner of the device nothing.' The casino buys your excitement for free. You pay for it.

Online multi-line machines add a second trick: the 'loss disguised as a win.' You bet a dollar, get ten cents back — and the machine flashes and chimes like you won. Your brain counts it as a win. Your wallet counts it as a loss.

THE RED FLAG

Brain scans show near misses fire the same dopamine reward pathways as real wins — even though you got nothing (Clark and Chase, The Journal of Neuroscience, 2010). And the people who gamble compulsively react to near misses the most strongly. That 'so close' feeling is not a sign you're due. It's your brain's reward system being played.

Any game that makes losing feel like progress is engineered to keep you seated. If you find yourself saying 'one more, I'm getting closer' — notice it. Closer isn't real on a random machine.

THE RULE

Treat a near miss exactly as what it is: a loss with better lighting. Decide the most you're willing to spend before you start, and when that money is gone, the 'almost' doesn't count as information — it counts as nothing.

Never chase the feeling of being close. And if you want to see how these machines are built to keep you playing, read the House Edge guide at https://erawaeb.com/guides/the-house-edge/.

(Source: Casino.org, September 2026; research in The Journal of Neuroscience (Clark & Chase, 2010) and Trends in Cognitive Sciences (Peters, 2025).)
